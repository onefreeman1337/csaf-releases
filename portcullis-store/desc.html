Why the loops matter

22 of the 123 files are real loops with sample-accurate loop points, and they exist for one reason: the middle of an animation has no fixed length. If your portcullis takes 1.4 seconds to rise on one map and 3 seconds on another, a one-shot cannot score either of them — you cut it off or you leave silence. Here you play start, loop move for exactly as long as the thing is moving, then play stop.

Every loop was checked mechanically at the wrap for a click, for a level pump, and for a fade across its own length. They are built by folding the decay tail onto the head rather than by crossfading, because an equal-gain crossfade sums two decorrelated streams about 3 dB down and the dip is audible.

One stone keep, not a bag of dungeon sounds

This is not a generic catalogue. It is one cold building: oak beams, wrought iron, cut granite, iron chain, brass wards, hemp rope, spring steel, cage bar, dry bone and a lead counterweight, all moved through one shared room. A granite slab and an oak door struck the same way sound like different objects, and everything sounds like it lives in the same place. How it was made, and what is honestly claimed

Every sound is synthesised from code — hand-authored modal recipes for ten materials, each struck, dragged, wound or released. There are no samples in this pack and no third-party audio of any kind, so there is nothing here whose licence you inherit.

1138 mechanical checks passed with none failing: no clipping, no silence, no abrupt edges, duration sanity per slot, loop-seam continuity proven two independent ways, loudness coherence, spectral and duration variety, and a check that every declared state resolves to real, distinct audio. Those measure the signal. They are not a claim that a given sound is the right one for your game, which is exactly why the demo above is a real subset rather than a trailer. The measurements ship with the pack in pack_proof.json, with a sha256 per file, so you can check every one of them yourself.
