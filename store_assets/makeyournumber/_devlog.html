MAKE YOUR NUMBER is live, free, and playable in your browser.

You keep a lighthouse on a bad coast. Six vessels pass in the dark and each one is hiding what she is. You learn what you learn from where you point the lamp, because the beam lights one arc at a time and the haar takes the rest. When the sweeps are gone you name every berth and hand the log in.

Why a generated case rather than an authored one

The hard problem with a mystery is that it can be solved exactly once. That is why almost nothing in the genre supports a free browser game people come back to: the second play is not a game, it is a recital.

So every night here is drawn fresh — and then proved, before you ever see it. The generator draws a solution, builds the full clue pool, and refuses the night unless the solver reads back exactly one reading. It then requires the night to be closable by elimination alone, no case splitting, because the log you rule cells out in is the same grid the solver uses, and a night that needed a technique the grid cannot express would be asking you to do something the game has given you no tool for. Finally it strips clues away one at a time for as long as the night still qualifies, so nothing you are handed is decoration.

Those three guarantees are the product. The third image on the store page is not a claim about them: it runs the shipped generator and the shipped solver while the picture is being built and prints whatever comes back. The build refuses to write that image if the numbers come back false.

The part that surprised me

The first version had nights that were uniquely solvable and completely unfair. The collected evidence pinned exactly one answer while pure propagation determined nothing at all — zero of twenty four cells — which means the only route to the answer was to guess and check. That is forbidden by construction now, and it was found by a probe rather than by reasoning.

The difficulty dial was also dead for a while, and the measurement is the reason I trust the current one. A bot pointing the lamp at random was keeping 39 of 40 nights, and a keeper who had bought nothing matched a fully kitted one 40 to 40 — three safety margins had been stacked on the sweep budget. It now reads 40 of 40 for good play against 37 for random, and 39 for a bare keeper against 40 for a kitted one.

Being honest about that number: the lamp differentiates less than the design claims, and the difficulty genuinely lives in the deduction rather than in the gathering. A human playtest would be worth more here than another tuning pass.

Everything you can see was drawn by the game

No font file, no image model, no asset pack. Six seamless material plates, forty signal flags — the alphabet of the International Code of Signals plus its numeral pennants and substitutes, built from one shape language of bars, quarters, saltires, diamonds and balls — and eight rigs drawn as continuous outlines in two lighting states, so a barque and a collier are different shapes rather than the same shape recoloured. The lighthouse and its catadioptric lens are drawn in code at run time. The last image on the page is the whole set on one sheet.

One thing the game deliberately will not do: the picture never says something the evidence does not. A bearing holding an observation you have not read draws an indistinct shape, and a vessel is drawn with its true rig only when the observation is actually about a rig. A random identifiable silhouette would look better and would lie, and in a deduction game a picture implying an unjustifiable fact is unfair in the one way a player can never argue with.

Free, and it never makes you start over

A wrong name costs you the night, never the run. You keep what the Board paid you and every fitting in the lantern room, and a fresh night takes its place. Close the tab whenever you like — a night is rebuilt from its seed and your observations are replayed onto it.
