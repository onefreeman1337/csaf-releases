TIDEPOST is out, free, in your browser. You carry the mail between nine islands, and the sea between them is a real current field that moves with the season.

The thing I most wanted to get right is that the water should be an actual system rather than a picture of one. So the ocean is a stream function: a scalar field defined over the whole world, with the current taken as its perpendicular gradient. That sounds like a flourish and it is not. It buys three things a hand-drawn field of arrows cannot:

  • It is divergence free by construction. No cell is a source or a sink, water never appears or vanishes, and a drifting boat can never be sucked into a hole. Getting that right by tuning arrows one at a time is impossible; here it is free.
  • The pale lines drawn on the chart are the true streamlines. They are the level sets of the function the game is solving, integrated at draw time — not an artist's impression of moving water. The thing you have to learn to read is the thing the game is computing.
  • A season is one number. Move the gyres, change their strengths, and the whole field re-solves consistently — so "the same two islands are a different journey in a different season" is a property of the mathematics rather than a special case somebody wrote.

What the probes found before a pixel was drawn

Before any art existed, a headless probe played the whole voyage and tried to falsify the claim on this page, with a control arm that turns the current OFF and must report nothing. With the current on, 27 of 27 origin-and-season comparisons re-order the destinations, and one crossing costs 56.1 watches out and 13.4 back. With it off: 0 of 27, and zero asymmetry. So whatever the first arm sees is the current and not the search.

The campaign probe then forced three design corrections, and none of them was a bug:

  • The mail came out of the hold. When letters were a parcel you loaded at home, a competent policy sailed 69 legs and landed something on only a handful of them — the hold was empty most of the voyage and a landfall did nothing. A game whose ordinary move is wasted is not a game. You are the post boat; the sack is inexhaustible.
  • Supply decay became multiplicative. With a flat penalty each season against the lift a sack of letters gives, an island visited every single season converged to about 0.74 and never crossed the 0.75 threshold. The game asymptotically approached its own goal and could not reach it — invisible to every unit test, and a voyage that supplied all nine islands ended reporting zero standing.
  • Beacons exist because the back half had no decision in it. A competent voyage finished with 81 unspent goodwill and nothing left to want. A beacon is now the sink: it costs real goodwill, it can only go on an island you have already made whole, and it is the only thing in the game that removes work rather than adding it.

Everything you can see, the game made

44 distinct generated entities: six seamless material plates, nine island coastlines drawn in two states each, twelve chart marks and eight headings of one mail boat. Each coastline is a sum of three harmonics plus a headland lobe plus an elongation along the island's own axis, and the land inside every one of them is sampled from those same material plates — so the islands are made of the same stuff as the rest of the world rather than filled with a colour. The gallery plate "EVERYTHING IN THIS GAME IS GENERATED BY IT" shows all forty-four at once, and the counts printed on it are counted rather than typed.

The music and every sound effect are rendered from authored scores as arithmetic. No sample library, no model. The theme is built as a real loop: the render is trimmed to the score's own length and the discarded release is folded back onto the head, so the last chord goes on sounding over the first bar. Measured at the seam, the level difference between head and tail is 6.8 dB; the unfolded render measures 55.4 dB and is rejected by the same check — which is how I know the fold is doing something rather than being a nice idea.

Nothing here can be lost

No death, no failure state, no cargo that can be destroyed, no restart. An island's supply falls back between seasons but never below a floor it has already earned, so an island you once made whole is permanently better off than one you never reached. Run out of provisions and the rest of that leg simply takes twice as long — a setback, never a reset. A 300-leg randomised voyage asserts that floor holds on every island on every leg, because it is the promise the store page makes.

Two years of seasons, then an ending that counts how much of the coast is standing. Every ending is a real one. The worst honest outcome is a coast where a few islands were kept, and that is still a coast that was kept.

It runs on a phone. It saves after every action. It is free and it always will be.
